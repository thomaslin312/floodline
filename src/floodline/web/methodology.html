How floodline works

Where the data comes from, what happens to it, and how far the answer can be trusted. The limits come first, which is the order the model itself reports in.

What it cannot tell you

  • It is a screening model, not a hydraulic one. It assumes the water surface parallels the drainage line. There is no backwater, no levee, no culvert, no reservoir release, and no flow routed across a catchment divide.
  • It does not model storm surge. Coastal flooding is a different mechanism and none of it is here, which is why coastal survey marks are excluded from every accuracy figure rather than counted as misses.
  • Structure values are modelled, not appraised. The National Structure Inventory derives replacement cost from occupancy type, floor area and regional construction costs. Sound summed over tens of thousands of buildings; not sound for any one address.
  • The uncertainty band is a lower bound. It covers four named errors. It does not cover storey counts, floor area, class assignment, inventory completeness, the contents-curve library, or the structural assumption above.
  • An ungauged basin gets no damage figure at all. The depth map still runs, from an assumed severe-flood scenario that is labelled as such, but a building count and a currency total read as measurements however they are captioned, so they are withheld rather than produced from an assumption.

The core idea

Everything rests on one surface: HAND, the height of every cell above the drainage line it flows to. Once each cell knows how far it sits above its own reach, a flood becomes a comparison rather than a simulation. A cell is wet when the water level in its reach exceeds its HAND, and the depth is the difference.

The pipeline

Numbered because it is a real dependency chain. Flow direction is meaningless on an unconditioned surface, HAND is meaningless without a stream network, and damage is meaningless without a stage.

01

Ingest

The watershed boundary comes from the USGS Watershed Boundary Dataset at any hydrologic unit level. Elevation comes from 3DEP cloud-optimised GeoTIFFs, read over /vsicurl/ so only the tiles covering that unit are fetched. Everything is reprojected to a local CRS in true metres, chosen per watershed. A geographic CRS raises rather than silently distorting every area in the run.

io/sources.pyio/ingest.pyio/raster.py
02

Condition the terrain, then route it

Depressions are filled by priority flood with an epsilon gradient, then flats are resolved explicitly so every cell has somewhere to go. Without that step a quarter of one synthetic catchment drained into a flat and never reached an outlet. Then D8 flow direction, flow accumulation in Kahn topological order, stream extraction on an accumulation threshold, and finally HAND.

terrain/fill.pyflats.pyflowdir.pyflowacc.pystreams.pyhand.py
03

Turn a discharge into a water level

Discharge comes from a USGS gauge inside the unit. Where surveyed marks exist and agree on a year, the model uses that flood's peak rather than the largest on record, so the residual measures model error and not the gap between two events. That flow is transferred to every reach by drainage-area ratio, and each reach converts it to a stage through its own synthetic rating curve, built from channel geometry by Manning's equation. A Log-Pearson III fit places the flow in the gauge's own record.

hydraulics/rating.pystage.pyinundate.pyfrequency.py
04

Find what the water reaches

Structures come from the USACE National Structure Inventory: replacement value, contents value, occupancy type, storey count, foundation height and day and night population, per building. Footprints are clipped to the watershed polygon rather than its bounding box, a correction that removed 243,586 buildings from one denominator. A population raster gives people in flooded cells independently of the structure count, and the two disagree by about a third, which is itself the finding.

exposure/buildings.pypopulation.pyio/nsi.pyio/overture.py
05

Price the damage

Depth-damage curves come from the USACE go-consequences library: 42 HAZUS occupancy types, separate structure and contents functions, each with a published per-depth spread. They join to the inventory on occupancy code, which is the reason to prefer that pair over any other. Damage is a fraction of replacement value read at depth above finished floor, then summed. The whole thing is recomputed at every rung of the discharge ladder, so moving the slider prices a different flood rather than redrawing the same one.

damage/curves.pyusace.pyestimate.pyladder.py

Nothing is bundled with the code. Every input is fetched on demand and cached locally, which is why no result here can be one that was not actually produced. The Monte Carlo

A single damage total is a point estimate dressed as a fact. Every run therefore draws the whole calculation a thousand times, perturbing four inputs together, and reports the 5th to 95th percentile of the result.

What is perturbed, and how

TermHow it is drawnShare of the band
Gauge stageNormal, one shift per draw, applied to every reach100%
Replacement costLog-normal, one scalar per draw, so a draw cannot make rebuilding free84%
Curve choiceA published family per draw, plus each curve's own spread16%
DEM errorNormal, per building and independent, since lidar error decorrelates over tens of metres8%

The share column is each term sampled alone, as a percentage of the point estimate, against 123% for all four together. Stage and cost dominate because they change how many buildings are wet; the curve only changes what each wet building costs, and DEM error largely averages out across a basin. This corrects a claim carried in the code for months, that curve family was the largest term. It was reasoning, and it was wrong.

Curve choice is worth a note of its own. Published depth-damage families disagree with each other by roughly 2.3× at one metre above the floor, which is far more than any of them disagrees with itself. Loading a single detailed library used to switch that term off entirely, so the band came out tighter for having better curves. It now leads the sampling at 60% and the others share the rest.

The interval is reported at the observed discharge. Running a full Monte Carlo at every rung of the discharge ladder takes about a quarter of an hour and returns the same shape stretched, so the ladder carries point estimates only.

How accurate it is

Scored against USGS surveyed high-water marks in every watershed in the country that holds at least eight good ones: 16 basins, 1,287 marks, median RMSE 2.16 m, ranging from 0.97 m to 11.85 m. Three of the sixteen come in under 1.5 m.

Bias has no consistent sign, with a median of +0.11 m spanning −2.08 to +8.34, so there is no global offset to correct. The model is roughly unbiased across the country and unreliable in any one place, which is the honest shape of a screening method.

It leaves 53% of surveyed riverine marks dry. Those are places water demonstrably reached. And marks are a one-sided sample, because nobody surveys a mark where the water never came, so this bounds the misses and says nothing at all about ground the model floods that stayed dry.

Against the alternatives it is still clearly worth doing. On the reference watershed a constant stage taken from the gauge gives an RMSE of 7.86 m and floods 98% of the basin; a constant fitted to the marks gives 4.13 m; per-reach rating curves give 1.17 m while flooding 24%. A model that wets everything cannot be wrong about a wet mark, which is why extent is always reported next to error.

Damage has no independent ground truth at all. National flood insurance claims are compared as an explicit one-sided floor, never as a validation: a claim requires a property to be insured, flooded, and its owner to file, so the model sitting several times above the claim count is the expected direction rather than a result.
