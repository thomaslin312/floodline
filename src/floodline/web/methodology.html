How floodline works

A screening model, built entirely from derived and modelled open data, and resting on a good many assumptions. Read what follows with that in mind.

The core idea

Everything rests on one surface: HAND, the height of every cell above the drainage line it flows to. Once each cell knows how far it sits above its own reach, a flood becomes a comparison rather than a simulation. A cell is wet when the water level in its reach exceeds its HAND, and the depth is the difference.

The pipeline

01

Ingest

The watershed boundary comes from the USGS Watershed Boundary Dataset at any hydrologic unit level; elevation from 3DEP, read over range requests so only the tiles covering that unit are fetched. Everything is reprojected to a local CRS in true metres, chosen per watershed.

02

Condition the terrain, then route it

Depressions are filled and flats resolved explicitly, so every cell has somewhere to go. Then D8 flow direction, flow accumulation, stream extraction on an accumulation threshold, and finally HAND.

03

Turn a discharge into a water level

Discharge comes from a USGS gauge inside the unit — that gauge's peak of record, or, where surveyed marks agree on a year, that flood's peak instead, so the residual measures model error rather than the gap between two events. The flow is transferred to every reach by drainage-area ratio, and each reach converts it to a stage through its own rating curve built from channel geometry by Manning's equation.

04

Find what the water reaches

Structures come from the USACE National Structure Inventory: replacement and contents value, occupancy type, storey count, foundation height, and day and night population, per building. A population raster counts people in flooded cells independently of the structure count, and the two disagree by about a third — which is itself the finding.

05

Price the damage

Depth-damage curves join to the inventory on occupancy code: 42 HAZUS types, separate structure and contents functions, each with a published per-depth spread. Damage is a fraction of replacement value read at depth above finished floor, then summed — and recomputed at every rung of the discharge ladder, so moving the slider prices a different flood rather than redrawing the same one.

The Monte Carlo

Every run draws the whole calculation a thousand times, perturbing four inputs together, and reports the 5th to 95th percentile rather than a single total.

TermHow it is drawnShare of the band
Gauge stageNormal, one shift per draw, applied to every reach100%
Replacement costLog-normal, one scalar per draw84%
Curve choiceA published family per draw, plus each curve's own spread16%
DEM errorNormal, per building and independent8%

Each term is sampled alone, as a percentage of the point estimate, against 123% for all four together. Stage and cost dominate because they change how many buildings are wet; the curve only changes what each wet building costs, and DEM error largely averages out across a basin.

How accurate it is

Extent is validated. Scored against USGS surveyed high-water marks in every watershed in the country holding at least eight good ones: 16 basins, 1,287 marks, median RMSE 2.16 m, ranging from 0.97 m to 11.85 m. Bias has no consistent sign, so there is no global offset to correct.

Overall: reliable about where and how deep, unvalidated about how much. Extent and depth carry a stated error of roughly 2 m, and the building counts and loss ratio derived from them inherit it. The currency total has no ground truth behind it — against FEMA's own record by census tract it shows no rank correlation — so read it as a scale rather than a figure for any one neighbourhood. Underneath all of it the chain is screening-grade: one gauge stands in for a whole basin, structure values are modelled rather than appraised, and the uncertainty band covers four named errors rather than every error there is.
