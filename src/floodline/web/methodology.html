How floodline works

One surface, five stages, and a stated error. The limits come first, which is the order the model reports in.

What it cannot tell you

  • It is a screening model, not a hydraulic one. It assumes the water surface parallels the drainage line: no backwater, levees, culverts or reservoir releases.
  • It does not model storm surge. Coastal marks are excluded from every accuracy figure rather than counted as misses.
  • Structure values are modelled, not appraised. Sound summed over tens of thousands of buildings; not sound for any one address.
  • The uncertainty band is a lower bound. It covers four named errors, not everything that could be wrong.
  • An ungauged basin gets no damage figure. The depth map still runs from a labelled scenario, but a building count and a currency total read as measurements however they are captioned, so they are withheld.

The core idea

Everything rests on one surface: HAND, the height of every cell above the drainage line it flows to. Once each cell knows how far it sits above its own reach, a flood becomes a comparison rather than a simulation. A cell is wet when the water level in its reach exceeds its HAND, and the depth is the difference.

The pipeline

Numbered because it is a real dependency chain: flow direction is meaningless on an unconditioned surface, HAND without a stream network, damage without a stage.

01

Ingest

The watershed boundary comes from the USGS Watershed Boundary Dataset at any hydrologic unit level; elevation from 3DEP, read over range requests so only the tiles covering that unit are fetched. Everything is reprojected to a local CRS in true metres, chosen per watershed.

02

Condition the terrain, then route it

Depressions are filled and flats resolved explicitly, so every cell has somewhere to go. Then D8 flow direction, flow accumulation, stream extraction on an accumulation threshold, and finally HAND.

03

Turn a discharge into a water level

Discharge comes from a USGS gauge inside the unit — that gauge's peak of record, or, where surveyed marks agree on a year, that flood's peak instead, so the residual measures model error rather than the gap between two events. The flow is transferred to every reach by drainage-area ratio, and each reach converts it to a stage through its own rating curve built from channel geometry by Manning's equation.

04

Find what the water reaches

Structures come from the USACE National Structure Inventory: replacement and contents value, occupancy type, storey count, foundation height, and day and night population, per building. A population raster counts people in flooded cells independently of the structure count, and the two disagree by about a third — which is itself the finding.

05

Price the damage

Depth-damage curves join to the inventory on occupancy code: 42 HAZUS types, separate structure and contents functions, each with a published per-depth spread. Damage is a fraction of replacement value read at depth above finished floor, then summed — and recomputed at every rung of the discharge ladder, so moving the slider prices a different flood rather than redrawing the same one.

Nothing is bundled. Every input is fetched on demand and cached locally, which is why no result here can be one that was not actually produced.

The Monte Carlo

A single damage total is a point estimate dressed as a fact. Every run draws the whole calculation a thousand times, perturbing four inputs together, and reports the 5th to 95th percentile.

TermHow it is drawnShare of the band
Gauge stageNormal, one shift per draw, applied to every reach100%
Replacement costLog-normal, one scalar per draw84%
Curve choiceA published family per draw, plus each curve's own spread16%
DEM errorNormal, per building and independent8%

Each term is sampled alone, as a percentage of the point estimate, against 123% for all four together. Stage and cost dominate because they change how many buildings are wet; the curve only changes what each wet building costs, and DEM error largely averages out across a basin.

The interval is reported at the observed discharge only. Running a full Monte Carlo at every rung of the discharge ladder takes about a quarter of an hour and returns the same shape stretched, so the ladder carries point estimates.

How accurate it is

Extent is validated. Scored against USGS surveyed high-water marks in every watershed in the country holding at least eight good ones: 16 basins, 1,287 marks, median RMSE 2.16 m, ranging from 0.97 m to 11.85 m. Bias has no consistent sign, so there is no global offset to correct — the model is roughly unbiased across the country and unreliable in any one place, which is the honest shape of a screening method.

It leaves 53% of surveyed riverine marks dry, and marks are a one-sided sample: nobody surveys a mark where the water never came. So this bounds the misses and says nothing about ground the model floods that stayed dry.

The currency total is not validated, and fails its first real test. Modelled damage per census tract has essentially no rank correlation with either FEMA series for Hurricane Harvey — +0.025 against insurance claims paid, −0.055 against assessed individual assistance — while those two series, which sample almost complementary populations, agree with each other at +0.818. The signal is real and the model does not reproduce it. Building counts and the loss ratio are supported by the extent validation above; the dollar figure is a scale, not a number for any neighbourhood.
